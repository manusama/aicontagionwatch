The loop, traced

Much of the AI build-out is paid for by the same handful of companies investing in, lending to and buying from each other. If one link fails, the others feel it.

The IMF’s October 2026 Global Financial Stability Report reviewed 14 AI financing deals: about US$300bn of equity and US$650bn of debt, tied to roughly US$1.4tn of future spending commitments flowing back to the investors. It names an AI correction as one of three ‘salient risks’, warning of a ‘triple whammy’: equity falls, debt gets harder to service, and promised revenue fails to arrive.International Monetary Fund, Global Financial Stability Report, October 2026

Australian exposure: how it reaches us

Where a US AI bust would show up here, factor by factor. This is separate from the clock, which measures how close the financing loop is to breaking; this shows what a break would mean locally. Official figures come out monthly or quarterly, so each carries its own date.

Superannuation

Watch

~A$4.8tn, about half offshore

How it reaches us
Super is the main way an AI share-market fall reaches Australian households. About half of large funds’ investments are overseas, with roughly 31% in listed international shares, much of it in US markets where AI stocks dominate. Funds also hold growing unlisted assets, whose values are updated more slowly.
Where it stands
Balances hit a record of nearly A$4.8tn in 2025–26 and rose 5.2% in the June quarter as markets recovered. The RBA calls super a source of stability but wants funds to strengthen how they manage sudden cash calls, for example from currency hedges when the dollar falls or from members switching or withdrawing.
What to watch
APRA’s September-quarter statistics (December), and how funds handle currency-hedge cash calls if the Australian dollar drops sharply.

FY2025–26; allocation split Sep 2025. Next: APRA quarterly statistics, Dec 2026.

Inflation and interest rates

Stress

CPI 4.9%, cash rate 4.85%

How it reaches us
Two forces pull in opposite directions. The AI boom itself has added to inflation through global energy and equipment demand, and the RBA cites it directly. A bust would cool demand, but a falling Australian dollar, which tends to drop when global markets sell off, makes imports and fuel dearer.
Where it stands
Headline inflation jumped to 4.9% in the year to October, the highest since 2023 and above forecasts (trimmed mean 3.8%, up from 3.6%). Housing (+6.4%), fuel (+32.5%) and electricity (+25.2%, as last year’s power rebates dropped out) led the rise. The RBA raised the cash rate to 4.85% on 3 November, an 18-year high, in a 6–3 split vote. Even so, markets on 25 November priced about a 97% chance the RBA holds in December, and the Australian dollar fell to about US$0.659.
What to watch
The RBA decision at 2:30pm on 8 December, and whether fuel and power costs keep pushing inflation up while unemployment rises.

CPI Oct 2026; cash rate 3 Nov; market pricing 25 Nov. Next: RBA decision, 8 Dec 2026.

Government investment in the AI economy

Watch

Small direct, large indirect

How it reaches us
Government spending on AI itself is modest. The bigger exposure is policy that relies on private money: the National AI Plan (December 2025) aims to attract data-centre investment, and states compete for projects. If global AI investors pull back, promised data centres, jobs and grid upgrades could stall.
Where it stands
Direct commitments are small, such as A$29.9m for an AI Safety Institute. On 18 November Nvidia said it expects to develop up to 2 gigawatts of data-centre land, power and shell capacity in Australia by the end of 2027 with partners including Firmus and Sharon.ai, more than the ~1.6 GW the industry says exists today. That deepens the link to the US AI cycle. AEMO reports more than 40% of data-centre projects proposed since 2025 have dropped out or gone backwards in the grid queue. Construction work done rose 0.9% in the September quarter (building +2.4%), with JP Morgan pointing to strong growth in data-centre approvals and starts. The RBA’s forecasts assume no AI productivity boost and no regional data-centre hub.
What to watch
Whether Nvidia’s Australian plans get firm financing and grid connections, data-centre cancellations in Sydney and Melbourne, and any change to the National AI Plan.

Aug–Nov 2026; Nvidia plan 18 Nov; construction 25 Nov. Next: Mid-year budget update (MYEFO), Dec 2026.

Resilience of critical industries

Watch

Banks strong; energy tight

How it reaches us
Energy, food and finance are the systems people need to keep working in a downturn. Energy is tied to AI through data-centre power demand; food through fuel and fertiliser costs; finance through banks, super and private credit.
Where it stands
Energy: data centres use about 5 TWh a year (~3% of the main grid), forecast to reach ~34 TWh (~13%) by 2035–36; electricity prices are up 12.8% in a year and fuel 23.6%. Food: food prices are up 3.2%. Finance: the RBA says banks hold large capital buffers and could keep lending through a severe shock; private credit is about 10% of business debt and more exposed to property than to AI.
What to watch
Electricity prices and grid reliability over summer, fuel prices while the Strait of Hormuz is disrupted, any stress in property-focused private credit funds, and APRA’s planned 2027 stress test of how AI-driven economic change could affect financial stability (announced 12 Nov).

Sep–Nov 2026. Next: RBA Financial Stability Review, Mar 2027.

Employment

Stress

4.9% unemployment

How it reaches us
An AI bust would reach jobs mainly through confidence and spending: falling super and share balances make households spend less (RBA analysis finds a 20% fall in AI shares would noticeably cut household spending), and stalled data-centre and tech projects cut construction and tech hiring.
Where it stands
Unemployment rose to 4.9% in October, the highest since October 2021, as 43,500 jobs were lost (mostly part-time) and participation fell to 66.6%. Youth unemployment reached 11.3%, and Victoria and Tasmania are highest at 5.4%. Underemployment eased slightly to 6.2%. The rate is already close to the 5.1% the RBA expected only by the end of 2028.
What to watch
November jobs figures on 17 December, and whether rising unemployment shifts the RBA away from further hikes.

Oct 2026. Next: Labour Force, 17 Dec 2026.

Wages compared with prices

Stress

Wages 3.5% vs prices 4.9%

How it reaches us
Whether pay keeps up with prices decides how much buffer households have going into a downturn. Falling real wages plus 18-year-high mortgage rates leave less room to absorb job losses or a hit to super.
Where it stands
Wages rose 1.1% in the September quarter, the fastest in nearly three years, and 3.5% over the year (private 3.4%, public 3.9%), helped by the 4.75% award wage rise. But prices rose 4.9% in the year to October, up from 4.3% in September, so the gap between pay and prices has widened again to about 1.4 percentage points and real wages keep going backwards.
What to watch
Whether fuel and electricity costs keep running ahead of pay, and the December-quarter Wage Price Index in February.

WPI Sep qtr 2026; CPI Oct 2026. Next: Wage Price Index Dec qtr, Feb 2027.
